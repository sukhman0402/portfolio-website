01

Methodology

The instrument, the sample, and the analytical pipeline.

In plain English

We asked 72 vehicle owners 19 questions about how they maintain their vehicle: what they check, who they trust, how they react to warning signs, and whether they'd pay for a service that tells them what's actually wrong. We then tested which patterns in the answers hold up statistically, and which don't.

The instrument

  • 01
    Vehicle profile: type, age, weekly usage (3 questions)
  • 02
    Maintenance habits & awareness: service triggers, component-check frequency, record-keeping (3 questions)
  • 03
    The knowledge gap & emotional response: warning-light reactions, self-rated confidence, delay history (3 questions)
  • 04
    Sourcing information & trust: trusted source, frustrations, overcharging perception (3 questions)
  • 05
    Expenditure & consequences: self-diagnosis time, worry type, willingness to pay (3 questions, 2 free-response)

Analytical pipeline

  • •
    Data cleaning: multi-select explosion into binary indicators, ordinal recoding, a derived frustration-count and component-check composite.
  • •
    Exploratory sweep: a broad correlation/association pass across all variable pairs, used to select which relationships were worth formally testing. This was exploratory, not pre-registered, flagged honestly below.
  • •
    Bivariate hypothesis testing: 9 hypotheses (Spearman, Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, Chi-square), each reported with raw p, Bonferroni-corrected p, and an effect size.
  • •
    Multivariate modeling: logistic regression predicting delayed service; ordinal logistic regression predicting willingness to pay.
  • •
    Multiple Correspondence Analysis: mapping response categories into a shared 2-D attitude space.
  • •
    K-Modes clustering: categorical segmentation into owner archetypes; cost-curve model selection, K=3 retained.
Honesty note

Unlike a pre-registered study, the exact hypotheses below were chosen after an initial exploratory pass across the data, a standard and reasonable approach for a primary-research questionnaire feeding a design project, but worth naming plainly rather than implying confirmatory rigor it doesn't have.

Maintenance Behavior

What gets checked, what gets recorded, and what gets ignored.

How often key components get checked

Tire pressure is the one habit that sticks (78% check regularly); engine oil is the most neglected (69% check it only at service time, or never).

Record-keeping

Under half keep their own bills and logs; the rest depend entirely on the service centre's memory, or on nothing at all.

Vehicle age correlates with how often engine oil gets checked (ρ=0.337, pcorr=0.034). The line below shows the trend isn't perfectly linear: there's a dip at 2–5 years, a "honeymoon" period where a newer vehicle earns some complacency before age brings the habit back.

The Trust Gap

How owners react to the unknown, and who they believe when something goes wrong.

In plain English

When a warning light appears, most owners don't ignore it, but very few actually know what it means before acting. That gap between "I did something" and "I understood what I did" is where distrust of mechanics and service centres takes root.

Reaction to an unfamiliar warning light

Most trusted source

H8: suggestive (p_raw=0.022, not sig. after correction)

Owners who "know what the light means, then take it to the mechanic" also check their components most often (mean composite 6.7 of 8); the savviest reaction correlates with the most diligent baseline habit. Owners who ignore warning lights check components least (5.2 of 8).

Component check-diligence by warning-light reaction

H2 & H3: suggestive, non-monotonic (not sig. after correction)

Both willingness-to-pay and feeling overcharged peak in the middle diagnosis-effort bracket (1–2 hours), not at the extremes. That's why a straight-line correlation test (Kendall) found nothing here, while a group-difference test (Kruskal-Wallis) did; the relationship isn't linear, it's a hump.

Feeling overcharged → time to diagnose → willingness to pay

Each ribbon is a group of owners who gave the same three answers, coloured by their final answer (darker = would pay). The node lengths are the answer counts: 8 rarely, 44 sometimes and 20 frequently feel overcharged; 15 diagnose in under 30 minutes, 20 in 1 to 2 hours, 37 over a few days.

05

Frustrations

Five candidate pain points, and whether they travel together.

Frustration factors, and which ones are chosen together

Not knowing if replaced parts were actually faulty ties with waiting at the workshop as the most common frustration (50% each): a verification problem as much as a convenience problem. Ribbon width is the number of owners who chose both; wait time and faulty-parts doubt are the pair chosen together most (14 owners), just ahead of faulty-parts doubt and hidden costs (13).

H7.1–5: all not significant

None of the five individual frustration factors, tested alone, predicts willingness to pay (all p_corr > 0.5). No single pain point converts on its own, which is exactly why the multivariate model below matters more than any one bivariate test.

06

Hypothesis Testing

Nine exploratory hypotheses, Bonferroni-corrected, with effect sizes for every test.

In plain English

Testing 9 things at once means some will look "significant" by chance alone. The Bonferroni correction raises the bar to compensate. Only one hypothesis survives it here; that's a real, modest-sample finding, not a failure of the analysis.

Nine hypotheses, one evidence fan

Spoke length is the strength of evidence (−log₁₀ p). Only a spoke that crosses the dark ring survives the correction for running nine tests; H1 is the only one.

What actually holds up

Only H1 (vehicle age → oil-check frequency) survives correction as an isolated bivariate test. But three findings recur across independent tests (Spearman, Kruskal-Wallis, and the regression below) pointing at the same pattern: diagnosis effort, feeling overcharged, and willingness to pay are entangled, just not in a simple straight line.

07

Multivariate Models

When every predictor competes at once, what actually survives?

In plain English

A bivariate test looks at one relationship in isolation. Regression makes predictors compete for credit. Here: does anything predict whether someone delays a service, once other factors are accounted for? And separately, what actually predicts willingness to pay?

Model 1: Who delays a service?Logistic regression

Pseudo-R² (Nagelkerke) = 0.062 · none of the four predictors reach conventional significance. Component check-diligence trends toward reducing delay (p=0.094) but the model is honestly weak; delay isn't well explained by attitude or age alone.

Model 2: Who's willing to pay?Ordinal logistic regression

Two real signals, pulling opposite ways: feeling overcharged predicts more willingness to pay (β=+0.94, p=0.040): distrust sells the tool. But a higher frustration count predicts less willingness to pay (β=−0.58, p=0.033): too many compounding annoyances breeds fatigue, not conversion.

Design implication

This is the single most useful finding in the dataset. It means messaging should lead with a specific distrust: "verify you're not being overcharged", rather than a generic list of pain points. Piling on frustration framing risks talking someone out of paying, not into it.

Multiple Correspondence Analysis

Mapping every answer category into one shared attitude space.

In plain English

MCA places answer categories near each other when the same people tend to choose them together. Reading the map: points far from the center and far from everything else represent an unusual, isolated response pattern.

Reading the map

Dim 1 = 11.7% inertia · Dim 2 = 10.8% (2-D total 22.5%, modest, as expected for 8 categorical variables at N=72; read neighborhoods, not precise distances.)

The far-right outlier: "Ignores if it feels fine" sits alone at the extreme edge, closest to "Pay: No" and "Rarely feels overcharged": a small, genuinely disengaged cluster with no felt need for any solution.

The engaged-distrust cluster: "Frequently overcharged," "Trusts OEM centre," and "Trusts online forums" sit near each other on the opposite side: people who are engaged enough to seek outside verification and distrustful enough to want it.

The local-relationship cluster: "Trusts local mechanic" sits closer to the disengaged pole than the engaged one; a real, structural finding: trusting a specific person substitutes for wanting a verification system, it doesn't lead toward it.

Limitations

What this survey can't claim, honestly.

  • Modest sample, exploratory framing. N=72 with 9 tests means most raw-significant findings don't survive Bonferroni correction. Treat anything marked "suggestive" as a direction for further research, not a confirmed effect.
  • Not pre-registered. Hypotheses were selected after an initial exploratory sweep across the data, appropriate for primary UX research, but a different (weaker) evidentiary standard than a pre-registered confirmatory study.
  • Self-reported, cross-sectional data. Diagnosis time, check frequency, and delay history are recalled estimates, not logged behavior.
  • Convenience sample. Recruited through the same network as the original Drive Wise project; demographic and regional representativeness is unverified.
  • Modest MCA inertia. The 2-D map explains 22.5% of variance, normal for MCA with 8 categorical variables, but read it for neighborhoods, not precise distances.
  • K=3 from a cost curve without a sharp elbow. The three archetypes are a useful, interpretable segmentation, not a definitive typology.
  • Multi-select questions exploded into binary indicators. Co-occurrence patterns (frustrations, service triggers) are descriptive, not causally tested.
